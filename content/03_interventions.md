# Interventions: do not let one outcome wear another outcome's coat

(claim-c06)=
## C06 · No established routine medication for withdrawal

**Claim.** No medication has an established, high-certainty evidence base specifically for
routine treatment of amphetamine or methamphetamine withdrawal.

The 2009 Cochrane review found only four randomized trials with 125 participants and did not
establish a routine treatment [@shoptaw2009]. The 2023 methamphetamine-withdrawal review found
nine RCTs with 242 participants, but evidence was low or very low certainty and no medication
showed convincing routine efficacy [@acheson2023]. Contemporary reviews and guidelines reach
the same cautious conclusion [@li2023; @sinclair2026; @khalili2025].

Individual trials explain why the graph preserves uncertainty. A small mirtazapine withdrawal
trial found no significant benefit [@cruickshank2008]. A 1999 amineptine trial reported benefit
on reversed vegetative symptoms and global state [@srisurapanont1999b]. That positive historical
result is stored as a contradiction edge rather than erased, but amineptine is not a practical
modern standard and the trial does not overturn the broader low-certainty evidence base.

“No established medication” does not mean “no supportive care,” “never treat symptoms,” or
“all candidates are disproven.” It means the routine evidence claim remains unearned.

(claim-c07)=
## C07 · Four outcomes, four different questions

**Claim.** A reduction in stimulant use, improvement in withdrawal symptoms, treatment
retention, and functional recovery are distinct outcomes and should not be reported as
interchangeable evidence of success.

The ADAPT-2 trial used a toxicology-defined response for methamphetamine use disorder
[@trivedi2021]. The 2026 phase 3 mirtazapine trial found a modest additional reduction in
self-reported use days but no significant improvement in depression, insomnia, quality of life,
or oral-fluid outcomes [@mcketin2026]. Meta-analyses likewise report different effects across
use, craving, retention, withdrawal, and tolerability [@sharafi2024; @khalili2025;
@bastien2025]. Psychosocial evidence often centers on abstinence and dropout
[@decrescenzo2018].

These are all legitimate outcomes. The error is allowing a positive result in one domain to
masquerade as recovery in every domain.

(claim-c08)=
## C08 · Contingency management leads the use-disorder evidence, not the withdrawal evidence

**Claim.** Contingency management has the strongest current guideline support among
psychosocial treatments for stimulant use disorder outcomes, but this does not directly
establish efficacy for acute withdrawal symptom relief.

ASAM/AAAP identifies contingency management as a primary component of treatment
[@asam2024]. A network meta-analysis found contingency management combined with community
reinforcement performed strongly for abstinence and acceptability [@decrescenzo2018].
Broader amphetamine/methamphetamine networks still rate much of the evidence as low or very low
certainty [@khalili2025], and implementation remains a stated research priority
[@ballester2025].

The graph therefore labels the edge “stimulant-use-disorder outcome” rather than quietly
rewriting it as “acute withdrawal relief.”

(claim-c09)=
## C09 · The treatment forest is mostly low-certainty undergrowth

**Claim.** Across pharmacological treatment studies for amphetamine or methamphetamine use
disorder and withdrawal, most apparent benefits are supported by low or very-low certainty
evidence, narrow outcomes, or small trials rather than consistent improvement across
patient-important outcomes.

The broad network meta-analysis included 72 trials and 6,836 participants but found no
moderate- or high-certainty evidence for important improvement across patient-important
outcomes [@khalili2025]. Prescription psychostimulant evidence was heterogeneous, with a small
craving signal but no main-analysis improvement in withdrawal severity
[@sharafi2024]. Standalone naltrexone evidence remains low certainty
[@bastien2025]. Withdrawal-specific reviews remain constrained by tiny trials
[@shoptaw2009; @acheson2023], a limitation reflected in the 2026 BAP guideline
[@sinclair2026].

The recent phase 3 mirtazapine trial is a useful stress test: it found a modest reduction in use
days, no significant secondary-outcome improvements, and more drowsiness, weight gain, and
discontinuation [@mcketin2026]. That is neither “nothing works” nor “problem solved.” It is one
bounded result on one treatment target.
