# Withdrawal phenotype and trajectory

(claim-c03)=
## C03 · The acute syndrome is multidimensional

**Claim.** Acute stimulant withdrawal commonly presents as a multidimensional cluster involving
dysphoria or depressive symptoms, fatigue or low energy, altered sleep and appetite, anxiety or
irritability, craving, and psychomotor change, with marked variation across people and studies.

Clinical reviews and guidelines describe a symptom cluster rather than one canonical marker
[@li2023; @asam2024; @sinclair2026]. In McGregor’s small methamphetamine inpatient cohort,
increased sleeping and eating, depression-related symptoms, anxiety, and craving changed at
different intensities [@mcgregor2005]. Direct amphetamine studies add affective and sleep
evidence [@watson1972; @oswald1963], while retrospective interviews show broader lived
withdrawal and coping problems [@cantwell1998]. The AWQ is one attempt to make that cluster
measurable [@srisurapanont1999a].

A syndrome-level description is useful for research. It is not a substitute for individual
assessment, particularly when severe depression, suicidality, psychosis, agitation, prolonged
sleep deprivation, or medical complications may be present.

(claim-c04)=
## C04 · The first week matters, but it is not a universal clock

**Claim.** Small inpatient methamphetamine studies suggest that overall withdrawal severity
often peaks early and improves substantially during the first week, but direct amphetamine
sleep studies show a more complex course and the first-week pattern should not be treated as a
universal timetable.

McGregor et al. followed 21 methamphetamine-dependent inpatients for three weeks and reported a
high initial peak within 24 hours, near-control overall severity by the end of week one, and a
lower-level subacute phase [@mcgregor2005]. Zorick et al. found that average depressive and
psychotic symptoms largely improved during the first week, while craving followed a different
course [@zorick2010].

Direct amphetamine sleep evidence is the useful splinter in this tidy story. Gossop et al.
observed initial oversleeping, then substantially reduced and disturbed sleep toward the end of
week one that persisted through the 20-day study [@gossop1982]. Older physiological studies
also described slower normalization of sleep-related measures [@oswald1963; @watson1972].
ORAtlas therefore stores Gossop as a qualified contradiction edge: it challenges the
one-number “overall severity” curve without invalidating the methamphetamine studies.

The safest translation is “many symptoms may change rapidly during week one,” not “everyone is
recovered by day seven.”

(claim-c05)=
## C05 · Post-acute persistence is plausible, heterogeneous, and poorly quantified

**Claim.** Craving, sleep disturbance, mood symptoms, and related impairment may persist beyond
the initial crash for some people, but the prevalence, causal attribution, and
substance-specific duration of post-acute symptoms remain poorly quantified.

Zorick et al. observed craving throughout five weeks even after average depression and psychosis
improved [@zorick2010]. Li and Shoptaw organize psychostimulant withdrawal into acute, early
protracted, and late protracted phases [@li2023]. ASAM/AAAP guidance notes that depression,
anxiety, insomnia, and paranoia may continue for weeks to months in some cases [@asam2024].

The direct amphetamine evidence is older but important. Sleep disturbance persisted through
day 20 in Gossop’s study [@gossop1982], and Oswald and Thacore described several weeks before
sleep physiology normalized in six participants [@oswald1963]. Watson’s affective and sleep
findings also support a trajectory that cannot be reduced to one crash score [@watson1972].
Cantwell and McBride add retrospective evidence that symptoms can shape self-detoxification and
return to use [@cantwell1998].

Causal attribution remains difficult. Persistent fatigue or low mood may reflect withdrawal,
sleep debt, an independent mood disorder, nutritional disruption, medication effects, chronic
stress, another substance, or several mechanisms at once. “Protracted withdrawal” is best
treated as a time-bounded research hypothesis with competing explanations.
