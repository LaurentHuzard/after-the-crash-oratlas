# After the Crash

:::{admonition} AI-assisted pilot
:class: warning

This evidence map was produced with substantial AI assistance under human direction. It is
awaiting human scientific review. The mapped claims and TRUST assessments are structured
hypotheses and source-relation evaluations, not established consensus.
:::

:::{admonition} Safety boundary
:class: danger

This is a research artifact, not medical advice. Severe depression, suicidality, psychosis,
agitation, chest pain, neurological symptoms, inability to care for oneself, or other urgent
symptoms require professional or emergency assessment.
:::

## Abstract

Research on stimulant withdrawal is often flattened into one category even when the underlying
exposure is methamphetamine, non-methamphetamine amphetamine, cocaine, or a pooled sample.
This pilot evidence map separates those sources and examines acute symptoms, early and
post-acute trajectories, pharmacological and psychosocial interventions, measurement validity,
psychiatric overlap, and functional recovery. It contains 12 qualified claims,
22 structured sources, 77 claim–citation relations, and
relation-level TRUST assessments. Direct amphetamine evidence was located, including historical
studies of affect, sleep, and self-detoxification, but contemporary prospective trajectory
evidence remains heavily weighted toward methamphetamine. The mapped intervention literature
does not establish a high-certainty routine medication for withdrawal. It also shows why
reduction in use, symptom relief, retention, and rebuilding a workable daily life must remain
separate outcomes. This first release is a transparent pilot for ORAtlas, not a systematic
review, clinical guideline, or individualized treatment recommendation.

**Keywords:** amphetamine; methamphetamine; stimulant withdrawal; protracted withdrawal;
functional recovery; addiction; evidence mapping

## Research question

> Among adults who stop or substantially reduce sustained non-medical amphetamine or
> methamphetamine use, what is the time course of acute and post-acute withdrawal, which
> factors complicate attribution, and which interventions improve withdrawal or
> patient-important recovery outcomes?
